Retirement Systems Around the World

"Save for retirement" means something structurally different depending on where you are. Five systems, compared on how they actually work — not just the name.

United States · 401(k) + IRA

Entirely voluntary — nothing requires participation. A 401(k) is offered through your employer; you elect a contribution percentage, often with a partial employer match (e.g. 50% of what you contribute, up to 6% of salary — "free money" up to that point, since not claiming it is leaving part of your compensation unclaimed). An IRA is a separate account you open yourself, with its own lower contribution limit. Both come in Traditional (contribute pre-tax, pay tax on withdrawal) or Roth (contribute after-tax, withdraw tax-free) versions. Standard access age is 59½ — earlier withdrawals typically face a 10% penalty plus tax, with some exceptions.

Australia · Superannuation

The structural outlier here: participation isn't a choice. Employers are legally required to pay a percentage of your wages — the "Superannuation Guarantee," currently in the ~11–12% range and has been rising over time — into a super fund of your choosing, on top of your salary rather than deducted from it. Concessional (pre-tax) contributions are taxed at a flat 15% going in, generally lower than most people's marginal income tax rate. Funds are locked until your "preservation age" (around 60, depending on birth year), with narrow early-access exceptions like severe financial hardship or terminal illness.

United Kingdom · Workplace Pension

Employees are automatically enrolled by their employer (you can opt out) into a workplace pension, with a minimum combined employee + employer contribution around 8% of qualifying earnings — split varies by scheme, commonly close to 5% employee / 3% employer. Contributions get tax relief, effectively reducing their real cost. This sits alongside a separate State Pension, funded by National Insurance contributions over your working life — the two are easy to conflate but are genuinely different layers. Normal access age is currently 55, rising to 57 from 2028.

Canada · RRSP / TFSA

Two separate voluntary accounts, no national employer mandate (though employer-run group RRSPs exist). An RRSP works like the US Traditional 401(k)/IRA — pre-tax in, taxed on withdrawal, room based on roughly 18% of prior-year earned income up to an annual cap. A TFSA is more unusual: contributions are after-tax, but growth AND withdrawals are entirely tax-free, and unused contribution room carries forward indefinitely with no retirement-specific access restriction at all — it can be used for any goal, not just retirement.

New Zealand · KiwiSaver

New employees are auto-enrolled (with an opt-out window); you choose a contribution rate between roughly 3% and 10% of pay, your employer must contribute a minimum of about 3%, and the government adds a small annual member tax credit for eligible contributors. Funds are generally locked until age 65, but with a notable exception: KiwiSaver savings can be withdrawn early to help fund a first home deposit, making it double as a house-deposit vehicle for many younger members in a way most of the other systems here don't support.
